Seekers vs. Avoiders: Does Your Child Crash into Walls or Hide Under Tables?

AS
Avery Sage
Student Success Coach
Updated October 2025

Key Insights

  • Sensory seekers crave intense input; avoiders withdraw from overwhelming sensations.
  • Proprioceptive needs vary dramatically—some need heavy work, others need quiet space.
  • Misidentifying sensory patterns leads to ineffective strategies.
  • Understanding your child's sensory profile reduces family stress and improves daily flow.

You've witnessed both extremes in your household: one moment your child crashes into furniture with the force of a linebacker, seeking physical contact and movement. The next, they're hiding under the dining table, covering their ears, and refusing to emerge into what seems like a perfectly normal environment.

This isn't inconsistent behavior or manipulation—it's the manifestation of two distinct sensory processing patterns. Understanding whether your child seeks or avoids sensory input transforms confusing contradictions into actionable insights for supporting their needs.

The Sensory Balance Principle

Every person has an optimal level of sensory input for peak functioning. Some children need more input to reach this zone; others need less to avoid overwhelming their systems.

Understanding Proprioceptive Processing

The foundation of sensory seeking and avoiding involves Proprioception—the body's internal GPS system that tells us where our body parts are in space.

Proprioceptive Seeking vs. Avoiding

Sensory Seekers

  • Under-responsive to input
  • Need intense sensations to feel grounded
  • Crave deep pressure and heavy work
  • Appear "accidentally" rough

Sensory Avoiders

  • Over-responsive to input
  • Feel sensations too intensely
  • Withdraw from touch and chaos
  • Appear "accidentally" fragile

Recognizing Sensory Seeking Patterns

Look for these characteristic behaviors:

Physical Seeking Behaviors

  • Movement Craving: Constantly running, jumping, climbing, crashing into objects.
  • Touch Seeking: Hugging people tightly, leaning against walls, seeking physical contact.
  • Oral Seeking: Chewing on clothing, pencils, or non-food items.

Self-Regulation Strategies

  • Heavy Work: Carrying heavy objects, pushing/pulling furniture.
  • Compression: Wearing tight clothing, squeezing into small spaces.
  • Resistance Activities: Wrestling, roughhousing.

Identifying Sensory Avoiding Patterns

Watch for these withdrawal behaviors:

Environmental Avoidance

  • Space Management: Preferring small, enclosed spaces; avoiding open areas.
  • Touch Avoidance: Resisting hugs, recoiling from unexpected touch.
  • Noise Sensitivity: Covering ears, seeking quiet spaces, avoiding crowds.

Discovery Question

Ask: "Does my child seem to need more sensory input to feel regulated, or do they seem overwhelmed by typical sensory experiences?" This distinction guides your support strategy.

Effective Input Strategies

Tailor your approach to their sensory needs:

For Sensory Seekers

  • Heavy Work: Pushing laundry baskets, carrying groceries, wall push-ups.
  • Compression Tools: Weighted blankets, compression shirts.
  • Movement: Trampoline time, obstacle courses.

For Sensory Avoiders

  • Gradual Exposure: Brief, controlled sensory experiences.
  • Escape Routes: Quiet spaces, noise-canceling headphones.
  • Predictable Routines: Consistent schedules, advance warnings.

The Bottom Line

Your child's sensory seeking or avoiding behaviors aren't problems to solve but differences to understand and support. Whether they crash into walls or hide under tables, their responses reflect genuine attempts to achieve balance.

Rather than fighting their sensory preferences, support their regulation through understanding and accommodation. This approach reduces daily stress for everyone while honoring their legitimate needs.
